## How to use this track

This track teaches the economics you need before a master's course in economics and finance, starting from nothing. It explains how markets set prices, how firms think about costs and profit, how money and inflation work, what GDP measures, how banks and central banks fit in, and how to read economic data. It prepares you for **Financial Management** (interest, inflation, exchange rates, markets) and gives you the vocabulary for **Econometrics** and **Microeconomics**.

### What you need first

Only the **Math Foundations** track: fractions, percentages, powers, and reading a straight-line graph. Every calculation here is small enough for a calculator.

### How to study

Each skill is a short lesson with a worked example, a few exercises and the answers (tap to show). Do the exercises on paper before you look at the answers. The skills build in order, but the last four (money, GDP, banks, exchange rates) can be read in any order after skill 6.

## 1. What economics is

This skill explains what economics studies, why every choice has a cost, and how economists think. After it you can name the opportunity cost of a decision and tell a fact from an opinion.

### Scarcity and choice

**Scarcity** means that people want more than the available time, money and resources can give. Because of scarcity, every person, firm and country must **choose**. **Economics** is the study of how people and societies choose when resources are limited.

A student has 10 free hours a week. Hours spent on a part-time job cannot be spent on study. Even rich people cannot buy more than 24 hours in a day.

### Opportunity cost

The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the best alternative you give up. It is not the sum of all alternatives, only the best one.

**Example.** You can spend 2 hours working at 10 euros per hour, or studying. If you study, the opportunity cost is 2 × 10 = 20 euros. If a course costs 500 euros and takes 100 hours that you could have worked at 10 euros, its full cost is 500 + 1,000 = 1,500 euros.

Money already spent and cannot be recovered (a **sunk cost**) should not change a forward-looking decision. If you paid 8 euros for a cinema ticket and the film is bad, the 8 euros is gone either way. The real question is what is the best use of the next 2 hours.

### Thinking at the margin

Economists look at **marginal** changes: one more unit, one more hour. A rational choice compares the **marginal benefit** (extra gain) with the **marginal cost** (extra cost). Do more as long as marginal benefit is larger.

The second slice of pizza gives less pleasure than the first. If a slice costs 3 euros and the second slice is worth 5 to you and the third is worth 2, you buy two slices.

Example: a factory can make one more table. It costs 80 euros in materials and labour and sells for 100. Marginal benefit 100 > marginal cost 80, so make it.

### Incentives and trade

People respond to **incentives**: rewards and costs. A tax on cigarettes raises the cost, so some people smoke less. A bonus for sales makes workers sell more.

**Trade** can make both sides better off because people differ in what they do cheaply. This is why countries and people specialise (see Exchange rates and trade).

### How economists work: models

A **model** is a simplified picture of reality. A map leaves out most details so that you can find the road. Models use **assumptions**. A common one is **ceteris paribus** (Latin for "other things equal"): to see the effect of price on demand, you imagine nothing else changes.

| Branch | Studies | Example question |
|---|---|---|
| Microeconomics | households, firms, markets | Why did the price of coffee rise? |
| Macroeconomics | whole economy | Why is inflation 6%? |

A **positive** statement can be tested with facts: "The minimum wage is 600 euros." A **normative** statement is a value judgement: "The minimum wage should be higher." Economics can say what happens if a wage is raised; whether it should be raised also depends on values.

### Exercises

1. You skip a job paying 15 euros per hour for 4 hours to attend a free workshop. What is the opportunity cost?
2. You paid a non-refundable 50 euro fee for a gym and then find you hate it. Is the 50 euros relevant to the decision to go tomorrow?
3. A bakery can make one more cake. It costs 12 euros extra and sells for 10. Should it?
4. Positive or normative? (a) "Unemployment is 5%." (b) "The government should reduce unemployment."

**Answers.** 1) 4 × 15 = 60 euros of lost wages (plus nothing else, because the workshop is free). 2) No, it is a sunk cost; only tomorrow's costs and benefits matter. 3) No: marginal cost 12 is more than marginal benefit 10. 4) (a) positive, (b) normative.

### Common mistakes

- Counting all alternatives as the cost. Only the best alternative counts.
- Letting sunk costs decide ("I paid, so I must continue").
- Mixing positive and normative claims: "the data show it is fair" mixes a fact and a value.

## 2. Demand and supply

This skill teaches how buyers and sellers together set a price. After it you can read a demand and supply table, find the equilibrium price, and predict what happens when something changes.

### Demand

**Demand** shows how much of a good people want to buy at each price. The **law of demand**: when price rises, **quantity demanded** falls (ceteris paribus). Reasons: people buy substitutes, and the good takes a bigger part of income.

A demand schedule can be written as a rule. Example for bread (loaves per day in a town):

```
Qd = 100 - 2P
P = 10 -> Qd = 80
P = 20 -> Qd = 60
P = 30 -> Qd = 40
```

Plotted with price on the vertical axis, this is a line that slopes down.

### Supply

**Supply** shows how much sellers want to sell at each price. The **law of supply**: when price rises, **quantity supplied** rises, because higher prices cover higher costs and bring more profit.

```
Qs = 20 + 2P
P = 10 -> Qs = 40
P = 20 -> Qs = 60
P = 30 -> Qs = 80
```

This line slopes up.

### Equilibrium

The **equilibrium** is the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Solve Qd = Qs:

```
100 - 2P = 20 + 2P
80 = 4P
P = 20, Q = 100 - 2(20) = 60
```

Check with supply: 20 + 2(20) = 60. If the price is above 20, there is a **surplus** (sellers have unsold goods) and price falls. If below 20, there is a **shortage** and price rises. At a price of 15: Qd = 70 and Qs = 50, so a shortage of 20.

A **price ceiling** (a legal maximum) below the equilibrium, such as rent control, causes a shortage. A **price floor** (a legal minimum) above it, such as a minimum wage, can cause a surplus (unemployment, in the case of wages).

### Shifts versus movements

A change in the good's own price moves you **along** the curve. A change in anything else **shifts** the whole curve.

| Shifts demand | Shifts supply |
|---|---|
| income, tastes | input costs, technology |
| price of substitutes or complements | number of sellers |
| expectations, population | taxes, weather |

**Example.** Incomes rise and demand becomes Qd = 120 − 2P. Then 120 − 2P = 20 + 2P gives P = 25 and Q = 70. Both price and quantity rise. If instead costs rise and supply becomes Qs = 2P, then 100 − 2P = 2P gives P = 25 and Q = 50. Price rises and quantity falls.

### Exercises

1. For Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = 20 + 2P, what is the surplus or shortage at P = 30?
2. Demand is Qd = 50 − P and supply is Qs = 10 + 3P. Find the equilibrium.
3. A new study says tea is healthy. What happens to the demand curve and the price of tea?
4. A frost destroys half of the orange crop. What happens to the price and quantity of oranges?

**Answers.** 1) Qd = 40, Qs = 80, so a surplus of 40. 2) 50 − P = 10 + 3P → 40 = 4P → P = 10, Q = 40. 3) Demand shifts right; price and quantity rise. 4) Supply shifts left; price rises and quantity falls.

### Common mistakes

- Confusing "demand" (the whole curve) with "quantity demanded" (one point).
- Saying a price rise shifts the demand curve; it moves along it.
- Reading the axes the wrong way: price is on the vertical axis even though quantity is the "result".

## 3. Elasticity

Elasticity measures how strongly buyers or sellers react to a change. After this skill you can compute a price elasticity, say whether demand is elastic, and predict what a price change does to revenue.

### Price elasticity of demand

**Price elasticity of demand** is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price:

```
E = %ΔQ / %ΔP
```

It is negative because price and quantity move in opposite directions; most people quote the size. Use the **midpoint method** so the answer is the same in both directions: percentage change = change ÷ average of old and new.

**Example.** Price rises from 10 to 12 and quantity falls from 100 to 90.

```
%ΔP = 2 / 11 = 18.18%
%ΔQ = -10 / 95 = -10.53%
E = -10.53 / 18.18 = -0.58
```

### Elastic, inelastic, unit

| Size of E | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| above 1 | elastic | quantity reacts strongly |
| below 1 | inelastic | quantity reacts weakly |
| equal to 1 | unit elastic | proportional |

Demand is more elastic when there are close substitutes, when the good is a luxury, when it takes a big part of income, and when buyers have time to adjust. Salt and petrol are inelastic in the short run; one brand of chocolate is elastic.

### Elasticity and revenue

**Revenue** = P × Q. When demand is inelastic, a price rise **raises** revenue because quantity falls by a smaller percentage. In the example, revenue was 10 × 100 = 1,000 and becomes 12 × 90 = 1,080. When demand is elastic, a price rise lowers revenue.

On a straight demand line, elasticity changes along the line. For Qd = 100 − 2P, use the point formula E = slope × P/Q, with slope −2:

```
P = 20, Q = 60: E = -2 x 20/60 = -0.67
P = 40, Q = 20: E = -2 x 40/20 = -4
```

At high prices demand is elastic; at low prices, inelastic.

### Other elasticities

**Income elasticity**: %ΔQ ÷ %Δincome. If income rises 10% and demand rises 15%, it is 1.5: a **luxury** (above 1). Between 0 and 1 is a **normal** good; below 0 is an **inferior** good.

**Cross-price elasticity**: %ΔQ of good A ÷ %ΔP of good B. Positive means **substitutes**: if the price of tea rises 5% and coffee demand rises 2%, it is 0.4. Negative means **complements** (printers and ink).

**Price elasticity of supply**: how much sellers respond. Supply is more elastic when firms can raise output quickly (spare capacity) and less when they cannot (farmland, in a single season).

Elasticities appear again in regression: in a log-log model the slope is an elasticity (see the Regression track).

### Exercises

1. Price falls from 50 to 40; quantity rises from 200 to 260. Compute E with the midpoint method and say if demand is elastic.
2. A shop raises price by 10% and sells 25% fewer. Did revenue rise or fall?
3. Income rises 8%; demand for restaurant meals rises 12%. Compute income elasticity. Luxury or necessity?
4. Gas demand: E = −0.3. A tax raises the price 20%. About how much does the quantity fall?

**Answers.** 1) %ΔP = −10/45 = −22.2%, %ΔQ = 60/230 = 26.1%, E = −1.17, so elastic. 2) Revenue falls: 1.10 × 0.75 = 0.825, down 17.5%. 3) 12/8 = 1.5, a luxury. 4) About 0.3 × 20 = 6% fall.

### Common mistakes

- Using the plain percentage instead of the midpoint, so the answer differs by direction.
- Mixing up "inelastic" with "does not react at all". It reacts, but weakly.
- Forgetting that elasticity is not the slope. The slope depends on units; elasticity does not.

## 4. Costs, revenue and profit

This skill shows how a firm counts costs and decides how much to produce. After it you can compute break-even, average and marginal cost, and the profit-maximising output.

### Fixed and variable costs

**Fixed costs** (FC) do not change with output: rent, a loan payment. **Variable costs** (VC) do: materials, hourly labour. **Total cost** TC = FC + VC.

**Example.** A bakery pays 200 euros per month rent (fixed) and 5 euros of flour and labour per loaf (variable). It sells loaves at 9 euros.

```
TC = 200 + 5Q
Revenue = 9Q
Profit = 9Q - (200 + 5Q) = 4Q - 200
```

### Break-even

The **break-even** quantity makes profit zero: 4Q − 200 = 0, so Q = 50 loaves. At 80 loaves: revenue 720, cost 200 + 400 = 600, profit 120.

Each loaf gives a **contribution** of 9 − 5 = 4 euros towards the fixed cost. Break-even = fixed cost ÷ contribution = 200 ÷ 4 = 50.

### Average and marginal cost

**Average total cost** (ATC) = TC ÷ Q. **Marginal cost** (MC) is the extra cost of one more unit. Example table:

| Q | TC | MC | ATC |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 130 | 30 | 130 |
| 2 | 150 | 20 | 75 |
| 3 | 180 | 30 | 60 |
| 4 | 220 | 40 | 55 |
| 5 | 270 | 50 | 54 |
| 6 | 330 | 60 | 55 |

(At Q = 0, TC = 100 = FC.) MC first falls, then rises as production gets crowded. When MC is below ATC, ATC falls; when MC is above ATC, it rises. So MC crosses ATC at its lowest point (here between 5 and 6 units).

### Profit maximisation

A firm maximises profit where **marginal revenue equals marginal cost**. A price-taker (a small firm that cannot set price) has MR = P. At P = 45, produce while MC is below 45: Q = 4 (MC is 40; MC at Q = 5 is 50 and too high).

```
Revenue = 4 x 45 = 180
TC = 220
Profit = -40
```

A loss, but if it shut down it would lose all 100 of fixed cost. So in the **short run** it keeps producing (loss of 40 is smaller than 100). The **shutdown rule**: stop only if price is below average variable cost. In the **long run** all costs are variable, and the firm exits if it cannot cover all costs.

### Accounting profit and economic profit

**Accounting profit** = revenue − explicit costs (what you pay out). **Economic profit** also subtracts **implicit costs**, the value of your own time and money. If you earn 50,000 from your own shop but could have earned 40,000 as an employee and 5,000 interest on your savings, economic profit is 50,000 − 45,000 = 5,000.

### Exercises

1. A taxi has fixed cost 600 per month and 0.50 per km. Price is 1.50 per km. Find the break-even km.
2. A firm has TC = 100 + 4Q. What is the ATC at Q = 25 and at Q = 50?
3. Using the table, at P = 55 what output maximises profit, and what is the profit?
4. You run a business with 80,000 profit but gave up a job paying 70,000. Economic profit?

**Answers.** 1) Contribution 1.00, break-even 600 km. 2) ATC(25) = 200/25 = 8; ATC(50) = 300/50 = 6. 3) Produce while MC ≤ 55: Q = 5 (MC 50). Revenue 275 − TC 270 = 5. 4) 80,000 − 70,000 = 10,000.

### Common mistakes

- Using average cost instead of marginal cost for the decision on one more unit.
- Treating fixed costs as something to avoid in the short run decision; they are paid either way.
- Forgetting implicit costs.

## 5. Market structures

This skill compares how competitive markets are and what that does to price and output. After it you can recognise the four market types and compute monopoly and competitive outcomes.

### Four types of market

| Type | Sellers | Product | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | very many | identical | wheat |
| Monopolistic competition | many | differentiated | restaurants |
| Oligopoly | a few | similar or different | mobile networks |
| Monopoly | one | unique | a local water supplier |

Barriers to entry (licences, big costs, patents) protect the few sellers in the last two.

### Perfect competition

Each firm is a price-taker: price is set by market demand and supply. Each firm produces where P = MC. In the long run, free entry pushes economic profit to zero, and price falls to the lowest point of ATC.

### Monopoly

A monopoly faces the whole market demand, so to sell more it must lower the price on all units. For a straight-line demand P = a − bQ, marginal revenue falls twice as fast:

```
P = a - bQ
MR = a - 2bQ
```

**Example.** Demand P = 100 − Q, marginal cost constant at 20.

```
MR = 100 - 2Q
MR = MC: 100 - 2Q = 20 -> Q = 40
P = 100 - 40 = 60
Profit = (60 - 20) x 40 = 1,600
```

A competitive market would set P = MC: 100 − Q = 20, so Q = 80 and price 20. The monopoly sells less at a higher price. The lost gains from the missing 40 units form the **deadweight loss**: ½ × (60 − 20) × (80 − 40) = 800.

### Oligopoly and strategy

With few firms, each firm's choice depends on the others. Firms could **collude** (agree to keep prices high), but each is tempted to cheat. This is the situation of the prisoner's dilemma, studied in Microeconomics (game theory). Competition authorities try to stop cartels.

### Measuring concentration

The **concentration ratio** is the combined market share of the largest firms. The **Herfindahl-Hirschman index** (HHI) sums the squares of all shares in percent. For shares 40, 30, 20 and 10:

```
HHI = 1600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3,000
```

One firm with 100% gives 10,000; many tiny firms approach 0. Regulators view high values as a risk to competition.

### Exercises

1. Demand P = 80 − Q, marginal cost 20. Find the monopoly Q, P and profit.
2. For the same market, what are Q and P under perfect competition?
3. Four firms have shares 50, 25, 15 and 10. Compute the HHI.
4. Why can a firm in perfect competition not raise its price?

**Answers.** 1) MR = 80 − 2Q = 20, Q = 30, P = 50, profit = (50 − 20) × 30 = 900. 2) P = MC = 20, Q = 60. 3) 2500 + 625 + 225 + 100 = 3,450. 4) Buyers can buy the identical good from many others at the market price.

### Common mistakes

- Using P = a − bQ as marginal revenue. MR has twice the slope.
- Saying monopolists can charge any price. They still face demand: a higher price means fewer sales.
- Thinking that monopoly profit is always large; costs and demand matter.

## 6. Money, interest and inflation

This skill explains what money does, how interest works, and why a euro today is worth more than a euro in a year. After it you can compute inflation from a price index and convert nominal rates to real ones.

### What money does

Money is a **medium of exchange** (you can buy things), a **unit of account** (prices are quoted in it) and a **store of value** (it keeps purchasing power until you spend it). Without money, trade needs a double coincidence of wants.

### Interest and compounding

**Interest** is the price of borrowing money. At rate r, 1,000 euros invested for t years grows to 1,000 × (1 + r)^t. At 5% for 3 years:

```
1000 x 1.05 x 1.05 x 1.05 = 1,157.63
```

Interest on interest is **compounding**. The **rule of 72** estimates doubling time: 72 ÷ rate in percent. At 6%, 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years (exactly 1.06^12 = 2.01).

### Inflation

**Inflation** is a general, sustained rise in prices. A **price index** measures it. The **consumer price index** (CPI) tracks the cost of a typical basket of goods. Inflation rate = (new CPI − old CPI) ÷ old CPI.

**Example.** CPI 2023 = 120 and CPI 2024 = 126. Inflation = 6/120 = 5%.

Money loses purchasing power: at 5% inflation, 100 euros buys 100/1.05 = 95.24 euros worth of last year's goods. **Deflation** is falling prices.

### Real and nominal

The **nominal** value is in current money. The **real** value corrects for inflation.

```
real = (1 + nom)/(1 + infl) - 1
```

A deposit pays 6% while inflation is 4%: (1.06/1.04) − 1 = 1.92%, close to the quick rule 6 − 4 = 2%. A worker's wage rises from 2,000 to 2,100 (+5%) while prices rise 8%: real wage change = 1.05/1.08 − 1 = −2.8%. The wage rose but purchasing power fell.

### Why prices rise

| Cause | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Demand-pull | total demand exceeds supply |
| Cost-push | input costs rise |
| Expectations | people expect rises and act on it |
| Money supply | too much money chases goods |

Low, stable inflation (about 2%) is a common central bank target. High inflation hurts savers and makes planning hard.

### Exercises

1. 2,000 euros at 4% for 2 years. What is the final amount?
2. At 9% interest, about how long does money take to double?
3. CPI goes from 150 to 159. Inflation?
4. A bond pays 3% nominal while inflation is 5%. What is the real return?

**Answers.** 1) 2,000 × 1.04² = 2,163.20. 2) 72/9 = 8 years. 3) 9/150 = 6%. 4) 1.03/1.05 − 1 = −1.9% (a negative real return).

### Common mistakes

- Subtracting percentages for large rates; use the exact division for big numbers.
- Comparing nominal values across years without correcting for inflation.
- Confusing a lower inflation rate with falling prices: prices still rise, more slowly.

## 7. GDP and the economy

This skill explains how we measure the size of an economy and its ups and downs. After it you can compute GDP from its parts, separate real from nominal growth, and read unemployment figures.

### What GDP is

**Gross domestic product** (GDP) is the market value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in a period (usually a year or a quarter). Only **final** goods count (bread, not the flour inside it), or we would count the same value many times. GDP counts production, not wealth, and it ignores unpaid work such as housework.

### Three ways to measure

| Method | Adds up |
|---|---|
| Expenditure | spending on final goods |
| Income | wages, profits, rents, interest |
| Output | value added by each sector |

All three give the same total. The expenditure view is the one used most:

```
GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)
```

C is household consumption, I is investment (firms' machines, buildings, new houses), G is government purchases, X exports and M imports. X − M is **net exports**.

**Example.** C = 600, I = 200, G = 250, X = 150, M = 200:

```
GDP = 600+200+250+150-200 = 1,000
```

Investment here means buying capital goods, not buying shares. Transfers such as pensions are not in G because nothing is produced in exchange.

### Real and nominal GDP

**Nominal GDP** uses current prices, so it rises with prices and output. **Real GDP** uses the prices of a base year, so it shows only changes in output. The **GDP deflator** = nominal ÷ real × 100.

**Example.** Nominal GDP is 1,155 and real GDP is 1,050:

```
deflator = 1155 / 1050 x 100 = 110
```

Prices are 10% above the base year. Going the other way: nominal 1,100 with deflator 110 gives real GDP = 1,100 ÷ 110 × 100 = 1,000.

**Economic growth** = % change in real GDP. If real GDP goes from 1,000 to 1,040, growth is 4%. **GDP per person** (GDP ÷ population) is better for comparing living standards: 1,000 for 50 people is 20 each.

### Unemployment

The **labour force** = employed + unemployed (people without a job who are looking). People who do not look (students, retired) are outside the labour force.

```
LF = labour force
unemployment = unemployed / LF
participation = LF / working-age pop.
```

**Example.** 90 employed, 10 unemployed, 150 working-age people. Labour force = 100. Unemployment rate = 10/100 = 10%. Participation = 100/150 = 66.7%.

Types of unemployment: **frictional** (between jobs), **structural** (skills do not match jobs), **cyclical** (not enough demand in a downturn).

### The business cycle and policy

Economies move in a **business cycle**: expansion, peak, recession (output falling), recovery. A common rule is that two quarters of falling real GDP is a recession.

| Policy | Tool | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal | government spending and taxes | government |
| Monetary | interest rates, money supply | central bank |

In a recession, the government can spend more or cut taxes, and the central bank can cut interest rates.

### Exercises

1. C = 500, I = 150, G = 200, X = 100, M = 120. Find GDP.
2. Nominal GDP is 2,400, deflator is 120. Find real GDP.
3. Real GDP goes from 800 to 824. What is growth?
4. 45 employed, 5 unemployed, 20 not looking. Find the unemployment rate and the participation rate (population 70).

**Answers.** 1) 500 + 150 + 200 + 100 − 120 = 830. 2) 2,400/120 × 100 = 2,000. 3) 24/800 = 3%. 4) Labour force 50; unemployment 5/50 = 10%; participation 50/70 = 71.4%.

### Common mistakes

- Reading nominal growth as real growth.
- Counting the unemployed as everyone without a job (students and retired are not in the labour force).
- Treating GDP as a measure of well-being; it leaves out leisure, inequality and the environment.

## 8. Banks, markets and central banks

This skill explains how the financial system moves savings to borrowers, and what a central bank does. After it you can describe how banks create money, and why bond prices fall when interest rates rise.

### Why a financial system

Some people save, others want to borrow to invest. The **financial system** connects them. Banks do this by taking deposits and making loans. **Financial markets** let people trade bonds and shares directly. They also share risk and set prices for money across time.

### Bonds and shares

A **bond** is a loan to a firm or government. It pays a fixed **coupon** (interest) and returns the **face value** at maturity. A **share** (stock) is a piece of ownership; it earns dividends (a share of profit) and price changes, with no promise. Debt holders get paid before owners if a firm fails, so shares are riskier and usually have higher expected returns (see Finance Foundations).

### Bond prices and interest rates

When market interest rates rise, existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive, so their price falls. **Example.** A one-year bond has face value 100 and a coupon of 5, so it pays 105 at the end.

```
Market rate 10%: 105 / 1.10 = 95.45
Market rate  5%: 105 / 1.05 = 100.00
Market rate  2%: 105 / 1.02 = 102.94
```

Price and interest rate move in opposite directions.

### Banks and money creation

Banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend out the rest. With a **reserve ratio** of 10%, a deposit of 1,000 lets the bank lend 900. That 900 is spent and deposited in another bank, which lends 810, and so on. The maximum total of deposits created = 1,000 ÷ 0.10 = 10,000. The **money multiplier** is 1 ÷ reserve ratio = 10. In practice the multiplier is lower because people hold cash and banks keep extra reserves.

Banks earn the difference between the rate on loans and on deposits. They face risks: if borrowers do not repay, or if many depositors withdraw at once (a **bank run**). Regulation (capital requirements, deposit insurance) tries to protect depositors.

### Central banks

A **central bank** (for example the European Central Bank, the Czech National Bank) controls the **policy interest rate** and so influences all other rates. Main jobs:

| Job | How |
|---|---|
| Price stability | raise or cut the policy rate |
| Financial stability | lender of last resort |
| Money supply | buy or sell bonds in the market |

If inflation is above target, the bank raises rates: borrowing becomes dearer, spending slows, prices rise more slowly. If the economy is weak, it cuts rates.

### Exercises

1. A one-year bond with face value 200 and coupon 10 pays 210 at the end. Price when the market rate is 5%?
2. Reserve ratio is 20%. What is the money multiplier, and how large can deposits become from 500?
3. Inflation is 7%, target 2%. What would the central bank usually do with the policy rate?
4. Why are shares riskier than bonds?

**Answers.** 1) 210/1.05 = 200. 2) 1/0.20 = 5; 500 × 5 = 2,500. 3) Raise it. 4) Dividends and price are not promised, and owners are paid after debt holders.

### Common mistakes

- Thinking a bond's price and the interest rate move together; they move opposite ways.
- Forgetting that the multiplier gives a maximum, not an exact figure.
- Mixing up the face value (what is repaid) with the price (what the bond costs today).

## 9. Exchange rates and trade

This skill explains what an exchange rate is, why currencies move, and why countries trade. After it you can convert prices between currencies, and decide which good a country should export.

### What an exchange rate is

An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in another. If 1 EUR = 25 CZK, a 10 euro item costs 250 crowns. To go the other way, divide: 1,000 CZK = 40 EUR.

When one currency buys more units of another, it **appreciates**; when it buys less, it **depreciates**. If the rate moves from 25 to 24 CZK per euro, the crown has strengthened: a 1,000 CZK hotel costs 41.67 EUR instead of 40.

### Who gains and who loses

An exporter earns foreign money and converts it. **Example.** A firm sells goods for 100 EUR and its costs are 2,300 CZK.

```
Rate 25: revenue 2,500, profit 200
Rate 23: revenue 2,300, profit 0
```

A stronger home currency hurts exporters and helps importers and tourists travelling abroad. A weaker home currency does the opposite.

### What moves exchange rates

| Factor | Effect on home currency |
|---|---|
| higher interest rates | attract money, strengthen |
| higher inflation | weakens over time |
| strong exports | strengthen |
| investor fear | weakens (money leaves) |

A **floating** rate is set by the market. A **fixed** rate is held by the central bank. Details such as purchasing power parity are studied in Corporate Finance.

### Why countries trade

A country has an **absolute advantage** if it makes a good with fewer resources. It has a **comparative advantage** if its opportunity cost is lower. Even a country that is worse at everything gains by trading.

**Example.** Country X makes 6 shirts or 2 phones per hour. Country Y makes 4 shirts or 4 phones.

```
X: 1 phone costs 3 shirts
Y: 1 phone costs 1 shirt
```

Y gives up less to make a phone, so Y has the comparative advantage in phones; X in shirts (1 shirt costs X 1/3 phone and Y 1 phone). Each specialises, then they trade.

### Trade barriers

A **tariff** is a tax on imports; a **quota** limits quantity. They protect local producers but raise prices for consumers. The balance of payments records money flows with the world: the **current account** (trade in goods, services and income) and the **capital account** (investment flows).

### Exercises

1. 1 USD = 22 CZK. How many crowns for 150 USD, and how many USD for 4,400 CZK?
2. The rate moves from 25 to 27 CZK per euro. Has the crown appreciated or depreciated? Who gains?
3. A firm exports for 500 EUR and has costs 12,000 CZK. Profit at 25 CZK per euro? At 23?
4. In X, 1 hour makes 8 cars or 4 bikes. In Y, 1 hour makes 3 cars or 3 bikes. Who has the comparative advantage in bikes?

**Answers.** 1) 3,300 CZK; 200 USD. 2) Depreciated; exporters gain, importers lose. 3) At 25: 12,500 − 12,000 = 500. At 23: 11,500 − 12,000 = −500. 4) X: bike costs 2 cars; Y: bike costs 1 car. Y has the comparative advantage in bikes.

### Common mistakes

- Multiplying when you should divide (check which currency is in the unit).
- Calling a weaker currency always bad. It helps exporters.
- Mixing absolute and comparative advantage.

## 10. Reading economic data

This skill shows how to read the tables and charts economics uses. After it you can compute growth rates, index changes and averages, and avoid common traps.

### Percentage change and percentage points

Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old. A rate that moves from 4% to 5% rose by **1 percentage point** (pp), and also by 25% (1/4). Say which you mean.

### Index numbers

An **index** sets one year to 100. If 2020 = 100 and 2024 = 130, prices are 30% higher. To compare with another base, **rebase**: divide every value by the new base and multiply by 100. If 2022 = 115 and 2024 = 130, then relative to 2022: 130/115 × 100 = 113.0, so a 13% rise from 2022.

### Growth over several years

The **average growth rate** over n years (compound):

```
g = (end / start)^(1/n) - 1
```

**Example.** 100 grows to 121 in 2 years: (1.21)^0.5 = 1.10, so 10% per year. The simple average of the changes can mislead: +50% followed by −50% is not a zero change (100 → 150 → 75).

### Logs and small changes

The **natural log** change is close to the percentage change when the change is small:

```
ln(1.05) = 0.0488  (about 5%)
ln(1.30) = 0.2624  (about 26%, not 30)
```

This is why economists often say "log points". Log changes add across periods, which is useful in regression.

### Averages and shape

Average income can mislead if a few people earn much more. **Example.** Five incomes: 20, 22, 25, 28 and 200. Mean = 295/5 = 59; median (the middle value) = 25. The median tells a better story of the typical person. The mean is pulled up by one value.

Always check:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Nominal or real? | inflation changes meaning |
| Per person or total? | population changes |
| Mean or median? | outliers |
| Which period? | seasons and base years |

### Data types

**Cross-sectional** data: many units at one time (1,000 workers in 2024). **Time series**: one unit over time (Czech GDP 2000–2024). **Panel**: many units over time. These three types return in Econometrics.

Common sources: national statistics offices, Eurostat, the central bank, the World Bank and the IMF. Charts can mislead through a truncated axis or a hidden scale.

### Exercises

1. A rate moves from 8% to 10%. Percentage points? Percent?
2. Index (2019 = 100): 2023 is 118, 2024 is 124. What is 2024 relative to 2023?
3. A price goes from 80 to 125 in 4 years. Average yearly growth (approx., use a calculator)?
4. Six wages: 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 83. Find the mean and median.

**Answers.** 1) 2 pp; 25%. 2) 124/118 − 1 = 5.1%. 3) (125/80)^(1/4) = 1.5625^0.25 = 1.118, about 11.8%. 4) Mean 150/6 = 25; median (14 + 15)/2 = 14.5.

### Common mistakes

- Reporting a percentage-point change as a percent.
- Averaging growth rates instead of compounding.
- Using the mean for skewed data.

### Mixed problems

1. Demand is Qd = 90 − 3P and supply is Qs = 10 + 5P. Find the equilibrium.
2. At the equilibrium price, E = slope × P/Q. Find the elasticity of demand there.
3. A café has fixed cost 1,500 per month, a variable cost of 2 per cup and a price of 5. Break-even cups?
4. Nominal GDP rose from 1,000 to 1,155 while the deflator rose from 100 to 110. What is real growth?
5. A saver earns 7% while inflation is 4%. Real rate (exact)?
6. A monopoly faces P = 60 − Q with MC = 10. Find Q, P and profit.

**Answers.** 1) 90 − 3P = 10 + 5P → 80 = 8P → P = 10, Q = 60. 2) −3 × 10/60 = −0.5 (inelastic). 3) Contribution 3, so 1,500/3 = 500 cups. 4) Real GDP now = 1,155/110 × 100 = 1,050, so growth = 5%. 5) 1.07/1.04 − 1 = 2.88%. 6) MR = 60 − 2Q = 10 → Q = 25; P = 35; profit = (35 − 10) × 25 = 625.
