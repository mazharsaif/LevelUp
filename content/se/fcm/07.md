## 7. Final review

This is the boss skill: a self-check, a one-page cheat sheet, and where to read next.

### Self-check

Answer out loud before you open the answers.

1. Why can a closed app receive a push, when it cannot poll more often than about every 15 minutes?
2. What is a registration token, and why is it not a user id?
3. A teammate says "we sent a notification message but our handler never fires in the background". Why?
4. Choose between Web Push, FCM and a foreground-service socket for (a) "your report is ready", (b) a walkie-talkie, (c) a PWA with no native app.
5. What goes wrong if you never handle `UNREGISTERED`?
6. Your payments app wants push for fraud alerts. What is the right architecture?
7. Does the user need a Google account to receive FCM pushes, and what was not verified?
8. What does using FCM commit you to, compared with adopting Firestore?

**Answers.** 1. The phone keeps one shared, OS-managed connection to Google. FCM sends down it and Android wakes your app on arrival, while polling is your own scheduled work, which the OS rate-limits. 2. An opaque identifier for one app install on one device. A user may have several, tokens change, and Firebase never knows your user ids, so your server stores the mapping. 3. Notification messages are shown by the OS tray when the app is in the background and do not invoke your handler; only data messages do. 4. (a) FCM or even plain email, because best-effort is fine. (b) A foreground-service socket for the live audio, with FCM as the wake-up. (c) Web Push. 5. Dead tokens accumulate, fan-out slows, errors pile up, and users with an old and a new install may get duplicates. 6. Keep the alert in your own backend (inbox, SMS or email as the source of truth), and use push only as a "check the app" signal with no sensitive content. 7. No, not on current Android: the token comes from the app's Firebase config and Play Services. A phone with no Google account signed in was not tested. 8. FCM commits you to a sender, a token table and Google as a transport. It does not touch your data or identity, so it is easy to remove, while Firestore or Firebase Authentication would own your data or logins.

### One-page cheat sheet

- **What it is:** Firebase is Google's bundle of app services. FCM is the free push transport from your server to an app install.
- **Use when:** you need to wake a closed Android app quickly and you already run your own backend.
- **Avoid when:** there is no Play Services, guaranteed delivery is needed, payloads are private, or you need live streams (push only wakes).
- **Key terms:** registration token; data versus notification message; priority and TTL; service account (HTTP v1); `UNREGISTERED`.
- **Gotchas:** use data messages for control; delete dead tokens; push is best-effort, so keep the truth in your database; keep the service-account key out of git; short TTL for time-sensitive messages and ask for notification permission.
- **Rule of thumb:** adopt FCM (the leaf), and think carefully before adopting Firebase's data and identity products (the roots). Push says "look now"; your backend says what.

### Further reading

1. **Firebase Cloud Messaging documentation** (firebase.google.com/docs/cloud-messaging): the primary source for message types, priority, TTL and the HTTP v1 API. Check it for anything tagged fast-moving.
2. **Firebase pricing page** (firebase.google.com/pricing): the authoritative answer on what is free on Spark and what needs Blaze.
3. **Android developer docs on Doze, App Standby and background-work limits** (developer.android.com): why high priority exists and why background work is restricted.
4. **dontkillmyapp.com**: a community list of phone-maker battery behaviour that delays pushes.
